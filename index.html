Constitutional Law

The Quiet Death of
Judicial Independence

A close reading of recent collegium controversies reveals a troubling pattern — the slow erosion of institutional autonomy dressed in procedural language.

AP
Ananya Pillai Guest Contributor · 12 min read · March 2025

The collegium system has long been the subject of criticism — from scholars who question its opacity, from governments that chafe at its autonomy, and from citizens who never quite understood how it worked. But recent months have seen something different: not an open confrontation, but a quiet renegotiation of the terms of judicial independence itself.

Consider the pattern. A name is recommended. Months pass. The government does not formally return the recommendation, but neither does it act on it. The name stays in limbo. Eventually, quietly, it is dropped or replaced. No explanation is offered. No procedure was violated. Yet something was lost.

The procedural frame

The Supreme Court's 1993 and 1998 judgments in the Three Judges Cases established the collegium as the primary mechanism for judicial appointments. The logic was straightforward: to insulate the judiciary from executive overreach, the power of appointment must rest with judges themselves. The government's role was reduced to a consultative one, its concurrence assumed once the collegium had spoken twice.

"The independence of the judiciary is not a privilege of the judge, but a guarantee for those who seek justice."

What the judgments did not anticipate — or perhaps chose not to address — was the possibility of executive resistance through non-action. There is no constitutional provision that compels the government to act on a collegium recommendation within any fixed time. The government can, in theory, simply do nothing.

What silence costs

The cost of this silence is distributed unevenly. Candidates who are recommended but not appointed face professional uncertainty. Courts whose vacancies are not filled operate under increasing pressure. And the signal sent to those who might be recommended — that elevation carries no guarantee, that considerations beyond merit may be at play — is difficult to measure but impossible to ignore.

None of this constitutes a legal violation. The government has not defied a court order. No contempt has been committed. But the question of institutional independence is not merely a legal one. It is also a question of practice, of convention, and of the accumulated weight of behaviour over time.

What we are witnessing, perhaps, is the slow redefinition of what independence means — not through doctrine, but through the accumulation of unremarked-upon departures from a norm that was never fully codified to begin with.
